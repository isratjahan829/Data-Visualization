\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Electroencephalography (EEG) emotion decoders must work for people whose data were never used in training, ideally on headsets with few electrodes, yet published cross-participant results differ in whether test-participant data are used. We benchmarked 26 models, including a two-stage contrastive model (CLISA-EWU), on four-class SEED-IV emotion recognition with repeated leave-one-participant-out validation (15 participants, three seeds) using 62 electrodes and a six-electrode frontal–temporal subset, scoring each held-out participant zero-shot and after calibration with 20 labelled windows (about 80 s). Zero-shot accuracy was modest (best 0.485, 95\% CI 0.428–0.542; chance 0.25), and CLISA-EWU did not differ from a two-layer perceptron. Calibration added 16–21 percentage points to contrastive, adversarial and pseudo-label models but at most 2 points to supervised networks. Calibrated CLISA-EWU reached an accuracy of 0.678 (0.641–0.718; Cohen’s κ = 0.57, MCC = 0.58) with 62 electrodes and 0.620 (0.590–0.648; κ = 0.49) with six, retaining 91.4\% of full-montage performance, and calibration made the model ranking consistent across participants (Kendall’s W 0.35 → 0.79). Brief user calibration, rather than architecture, explained most of the measurable gain.}
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textsf{\textbf{Keywords}}\quad EEG emotion recognition, cross-subject generalization, contrastive learning, few-shot calibration, wearable EEG, leave-one-subject-out validation

\textsf{\textbf{Research in context}}\par\medskip
\textsf{\textbf{Evidence before this study}}\par We reviewed studies of cross-participant (subject-independent) EEG emotion recognition indexed in IEEE Xplore, PubMed and Google Scholar, focusing on the SEED, SEED-IV, SEED-V, DEAP, DREAMER and FACED benchmarks and on domain-adaptation, contrastive and reduced-electrode methods \pending{document search dates, terms and the number of records screened}. \textsf{\textbf{Added value of this study}}\par In a benchmark of 26 models with 45 leave-one-participant-out runs each, zero-shot four-class accuracy was modest and no deep model was meaningfully better than a two-layer perceptron (equivalent within ±3 percentage points). Brief user calibration appears to be the main lever for practical systems, and low-density headsets look viable under calibration. External validation in independent and more diverse cohorts, real wearable recordings and calibration protocols that keep calibration and test trials separate are needed before deployment in consumer or clinical settings.\par\medskip

\section{Introduction}
Interest in objective affect sensing is driven by applications that range from adaptive human–computer interaction and neurofeedback to the continuous monitoring of mood in digital mental health, where self-report is intermittent and subject to recall bias. For such applications to be useful, a decoder must work reliably for a new user from the first session, with a headset that can be worn outside the laboratory and with minimal set-up. These practical requirements translate into two technical ones: generalisation across people and robustness to a reduced electrode montage.

Emotional states shape attention, decision making and mental health, and objective markers of emotion could support adaptive human–computer interaction, neurofeedback and digital mental-health monitoring\cite{russell,seediv}. Electroencephalography (EEG) is portable, inexpensive and has millisecond resolution, which makes it the most practical neural signal for everyday affect sensing\cite{seed,deap}. Two obstacles separate laboratory results from use. First, a deployed decoder must work for a person whose data were never used in training, but EEG responses to the same stimulus differ markedly between individuals because of anatomy, electrode contact, baseline rhythms and appraisal\cite{clisa,ganin,dan}. Second, research datasets are recorded with 32–64 electrodes, whereas wearable headsets carry far fewer.

Many methods target the first obstacle, including domain-adversarial networks\cite{ganin,bidann}, hemispheric-asymmetry models\cite{bihdm}, graph neural networks\cite{dgcnn,rgnn,sognn} and contrastive learning designed to make representations participant-invariant\cite{clisa}. Reported cross-participant accuracies on SEED and SEED-IV range from about 53\% to above 80\%\cite{dgcnn,dan,bidann,bihdm,rgnn,sognn,bfenet,attgraph,ssemerge}. These values are rarely comparable. Studies differ in whether unlabelled EEG from the test participant is used during training (transductive adaptation), whether a few labelled test-participant samples are used to adapt the model (calibration), how windows are split, and whether the reported figure is a mean over participants or a best fold. Splitting matters because features from adjacent windows of the same trial are strongly correlated, so any overlap between fitting and testing data inflates accuracy\cite{saeb,kapoor,liblock}.

We therefore asked three questions under a single, fully reported protocol. How well do classical, supervised, self-supervised, adversarial and semi-supervised models decode four emotions in unseen participants without any target data? How much does brief labelled calibration change this picture, and does the effect depend on the training objective? How much performance is lost when a 62-electrode research montage is reduced to six frontal–temporal electrodes compatible with a headband-style wearable?

We evaluated three hypotheses. First, that under strict zero-shot evaluation, representation-learning models would not substantially outperform simple supervised baselines. Second, that a short labelled calibration would benefit models trained with contrastive, adversarial or pseudo-label objectives more than purely supervised networks. Third, that a six-electrode frontal–temporal montage would retain most of the calibrated performance of the full 62-electrode montage.

This work addresses these gaps through a controlled benchmark with a rigorous, participant-level evaluation. The main contributions of this research are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Controlled cross-participant benchmark}: Ten classical and 16 deep models are evaluated on SEED-IV under the same repeated leave-one-participant-out protocol, giving 45 runs per model and electrode montage, with all per-fold outputs released.
  \item \emph{Zero-shot versus calibrated evaluation}: Every held-out participant is scored without any of their data and after calibration with 20 labelled windows, showing that the advantage of representation-learning objectives appears mainly after calibration.
  \item \emph{Wearable electrode reduction}: The same models are evaluated with a 62-electrode montage and a six-electrode frontal–temporal subset (FP1, FP2, F7, F8, T7, T8), and the retained performance is quantified at the participant level.
  \item \emph{Rigorous statistics and interpretation}: Participant-level bootstrap confidence intervals and paired Wilcoxon tests with Holm correction are complemented by class-level, representation-geometry and attribution analyses and a software prototype for wearable use.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on cross-participant and reduced-electrode EEG emotion recognition. Section 3 describes the materials and methods: the data, preprocessing and augmentation, the evaluation protocol, the baseline and proposed models, the training algorithm, the evaluation metrics and the statistical analysis. Section 4 presents and discusses the results, including their implications and limitations. Section 5 concludes the paper and outlines future work.

\subsection{Emotion models and EEG correlates of affect}
Computational studies of emotion usually adopt either discrete categories, such as the neutral, sad, fear and happy states of SEED-IV, or dimensional models in which emotions are located in a space spanned by valence and arousal\cite{russell}. The discrete view simplifies labelling with film clips, whereas the dimensional view underlies datasets such as DEAP\cite{deap} and DREAMER\cite{dreamer}. Both rest on the observation that affective states modulate cortical oscillations. The best-established correlate is frontal alpha asymmetry, in which relatively greater left frontal activity accompanies approach-related, positive affect and greater right frontal activity accompanies withdrawal-related, negative affect\cite{davidson,coan}. Beyond the frontal lobes, emotion-related changes have been reported across theta, beta and gamma bands and in temporal and parietal regions, and high-frequency beta and gamma activity over lateral temporal sites has been found to be particularly discriminative for emotion\cite{seed}.

Systematic surveys of EEG emotion recognition\cite{alarcao,jenke} highlight two consequences of this neurophysiology for machine learning. First, informative features are spread over many frequency bands and electrodes, which motivates multichannel spectral features and spatial models. Second, the same emotional stimulus evokes different spectral patterns in different people, so that a model trained on one group generalises poorly to another; this inter-participant variability is the central difficulty that the present study examines.


\subsection{Datasets and the cross-participant problem}
Public datasets have made controlled comparison possible. DEAP recorded 32-channel EEG from 32 participants watching music videos rated for valence and arousal\cite{deap}; SEED recorded 62-channel EEG from 15 participants watching film clips eliciting positive, neutral and negative emotions\cite{seed}; SEED-IV extended this design to four discrete emotions over three sessions\cite{seediv}; SEED-V added a fifth class\cite{seedv}; DREAMER used a 14-channel consumer headset\cite{dreamer}; and FACED provides 32-channel recordings from 123 participants with nine fine-grained emotion categories\cite{faced}. Because SEED and SEED-IV release precomputed differential-entropy features, they have become the de facto benchmarks for cross-participant studies.

In these datasets, each film clip yields many consecutive windows that share a label. This structure creates two distinct generalisation problems: generalisation to new windows or trials of a participant whose other data were used for training (subject-dependent), and generalisation to a participant whose data were never seen (subject-independent or cross-participant). Accuracies for the second problem are consistently and substantially lower than for the first\cite{jayaram,wu}, and it is the second that matters for deployment.


\subsection{Hand-crafted features and classical classifiers}
Early work relied on hand-crafted features. Jenke and colleagues compared a broad set of time-domain, frequency-domain and time–frequency features and found that band-power-related features and their combinations were among the most informative\cite{jenke}. Differential entropy (DE), the entropy of a band-limited signal, which for approximately Gaussian signals equals the logarithm of band power up to a constant, was introduced as a robust emotion feature\cite{duan} and outperformed band power in SEED\cite{seed}. Smoothing the DE sequence with a linear dynamical system further improved accuracy by suppressing window-to-window noise\cite{seed}, at the cost of introducing temporal dependence between neighbouring windows. Classical classifiers such as support vector machines, linear discriminant analysis and ensembles of decision trees remain strong baselines on these features\cite{lotte,xgboost}, and our own classical benchmark confirms that they carry emotion information across participants.


\subsection{Deep architectures for EEG emotion recognition}
Deep learning has been applied both to raw EEG and to spectral features\cite{craik,roy}. Convolutional architectures designed for raw EEG, such as the deep and shallow ConvNets of Schirrmeister and colleagues\cite{schirrmeister} and the compact EEGNet\cite{eegnet}, learn temporal and spatial filters end to end, and transformer-based models such as EEG Conformer add global self-attention\cite{conformer,vaswani}. For DE features, which are organised by electrode and band, graph neural networks have been particularly influential. DGCNN learns an adjacency matrix between electrodes\cite{dgcnn}; RGNN regularises this graph with anatomical connectivity and node-wise domain adversarial training\cite{rgnn}; SOGNN constructs a sample-specific graph\cite{sognn}; and hierarchical spatial–temporal models aggregate regional and global brain information\cite{regional}. More recent attention-based and frequency-band graph models\cite{attgraph,bfenet} and self-supervised graph models\cite{ssemerge} report the highest SEED-IV accuracies (Table~\ref{tab:lit}).

Two observations temper these advances. First, systematic reviews have noted that the reported gains of deep models over classical pipelines vary widely, depend strongly on the evaluation protocol and are often obtained with within-participant evaluation\cite{craik,roy}. Second, the architectural gains reported on SEED-IV are entangled with differences in evaluation protocol, as discussed below, so that the contribution of the architecture itself is difficult to isolate.


\subsection{Transfer learning and domain adaptation across participants}
Because inter-participant variability resembles a shift between data distributions, cross-participant EEG has been framed as a transfer-learning or domain-adaptation problem\cite{jayaram,wu}. Shallow approaches align feature distributions directly, for example with transfer component analysis\cite{pan}, and early EEG studies showed that personalising models through transfer learning improves accuracy on new participants\cite{zhengpers}. In brain–computer interfaces more broadly, Riemannian re-centring of covariance matrices\cite{zanini} and Euclidean alignment of each participant’s data\cite{he} reduce inter-participant shift with little or no labelled target data.

Deep domain adaptation extends these ideas to learned representations. Deep adaptation networks minimise a maximum mean discrepancy between source and target features\cite{long}, and their application to SEED and SEED-IV improved cross-participant accuracy\cite{dan}. Domain-adversarial training removes domain information with a gradient-reversal layer\cite{ganin} and has been combined with bi-hemispheric models\cite{bidann,bihdm}. Multisource transfer selects and weights the most similar source participants\cite{multisource}, and plug-and-play domain adaptation adapts a pretrained model to a new participant with a small amount of target data\cite{plugplay}. A simpler mechanism, adaptive batch normalisation, re-estimates normalisation statistics on target data without changing the learned weights\cite{adabn}. A common feature of most of these methods is that they use data from the test participant: unlabelled data in the transductive setting, or a small labelled set in the calibration setting. Their reported accuracies therefore measure a different quantity from zero-shot generalisation, a distinction that is rarely made explicit.


\subsection{Self-supervised, contrastive and semi-supervised learning}
Self-supervised learning offers a way to learn representations without labels. Contrastive methods such as SimCLR pull together two augmented views of the same sample and push apart other samples\cite{simclr}, while BYOL avoids negative pairs by predicting one view from another with a slowly updated target network\cite{byol}. For EEG, contrastive pretraining with signal-specific augmentations\cite{mohsenvand}, relative-positioning and temporal-shuffling pretext tasks\cite{banville} and transformer-based contrastive pretraining on large unlabelled corpora\cite{bendr} have all produced representations that transfer to downstream tasks. CLISA adapted contrastive learning specifically to cross-participant emotion recognition by treating the time-aligned responses of different participants to the same stimulus as positive pairs, explicitly encouraging inter-participant alignment\cite{clisa}.

Semi-supervised methods exploit unlabelled data during supervised training. Pseudo-labelling adds confident predictions on unlabelled data as training targets\cite{pseudo}, and MixMatch combines consistency regularisation, entropy minimisation and mixing\cite{mixmatch}. When the unlabelled data come from the test participant, these methods become transductive. Knowledge distillation\cite{hinton} has been used to transfer information from a large teacher to a compact student and is relevant when moving from dense to sparse electrode montages.


\subsection{Calibration, evaluation protocols and reporting}
In motor-imagery and event-related brain–computer interfaces, a short calibration session for each new user is standard practice, and transfer learning is primarily used to shorten it\cite{lotte,jayaram,wu}. In EEG emotion recognition, by contrast, results labelled as cross-participant or subject-independent may be zero-shot, transductive or calibrated, and the protocol is often described only implicitly. The amount of target data and how it is selected are rarely reported, even though, as the present study shows, they can dominate the outcome.

Data splitting raises related concerns. When windows from the same participant or trial appear in both training and test data, models can exploit participant identity or temporal autocorrelation rather than the effect of interest, inflating accuracy\cite{saeb,kapoor}. In EEG, block designs in which all samples of a class are recorded together allow classifiers to learn slow temporal drifts instead of class-specific responses\cite{liblock}. Finally, many studies report the mean of a small number of runs without participant-level uncertainty, and some report the best fold, which is not an estimate of expected performance. Reporting guidelines for prediction models, such as TRIPOD+AI\cite{tripod}, and statistical procedures for comparing classifiers across data sets\cite{demsar} provide a framework for addressing these issues that has seldom been applied to EEG emotion recognition.


\subsection{Reduced-electrode and wearable EEG}
Practical affective interfaces require few electrodes. Consumer headsets such as the 14-channel device used in DREAMER\cite{dreamer} and four-channel forehead-and-ear headbands have been validated for some research purposes\cite{krigolson}, and wearable EEG is an active area of engineering development\cite{casson}. In SEED, Zheng and Lu showed that selected subsets of four to six lateral temporal electrodes retained accuracy close to the full montage in subject-dependent classification\cite{seed}, consistent with the emotion-related information carried by frontal and temporal sites\cite{davidson,coan}. Knowledge distillation from dense to sparse montages has also been proposed\cite{hinton}.

However, most reduced-electrode studies evaluate subject-dependent performance, select electrodes using data from the same participants on which they are tested, or compare different models across montages. How much cross-participant performance survives electrode reduction when the same models and the same protocol are used, and how this depends on calibration, has not been quantified with participant-level uncertainty.


\subsection{Research gap}
Four gaps emerge from this literature. First, reported cross-participant accuracies mix zero-shot, transductive and calibrated protocols and are sometimes summarised as a best fold rather than a participant mean, so they cannot be compared (Table~\ref{tab:lit}). Second, no study has separated, under one protocol, how much of the performance of classical, supervised, self-supervised, adversarial and semi-supervised models (Table~\ref{tab:families}) comes from labelled data of the new user. Third, the consequences of temporal dependence between windows for calibration and validation are rarely considered. Fourth, electrode reduction is rarely quantified with the same models, the same protocol and participant-level uncertainty. The present study addresses these gaps with a 26-model benchmark that reports zero-shot and calibrated performance separately, uses participant-level statistics, documents the limitations of its own calibration protocol, and evaluates a six-electrode wearable-compatible montage under identical conditions.

\subsection{Study design and reporting}
This study is a retrospective secondary analysis of a public EEG dataset with a pre-defined evaluation design in which the participant is the unit of analysis. All models were trained and evaluated under the same leave-one-participant-out protocol, with the same features, splits and seeds, so that differences between models reflect the models rather than the protocol. The study is reported following the TRIPOD+AI guideline where applicable\cite{tripod}. No new participants were recruited and no participant-level data beyond the released features were accessed.


\subsection{Data description}
The public SEED-IV dataset\cite{seediv} was used. Fifteen healthy adults watched 72 film clips (24 per session, three sessions on different days) selected to elicit neutral, sad, fear and happy states while 62-channel EEG was recorded according to the international 10–20 system. The released feature set contains 37,575 four-second windows, 2505 per participant, with 10,170 neutral (27.1\%), 10,245 sad (27.3\%), 9225 fear (24.6\%) and 7935 happy (21.1\%) windows (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}); the classes are therefore close to balanced. Labels are assigned per film clip, so all windows of a trial share one label.

\subsection{Data preprocessing and feature extraction}
The EEG in SEED-IV was downsampled to 200 Hz and band-pass filtered by the dataset providers\cite{seediv,seed}. Differential entropy (DE) features were computed in non-overlapping 4-s windows for five frequency bands (delta, theta, alpha, beta and gamma). For a band-limited signal that is approximately Gaussian with variance σ², the differential entropy reduces to a function of band power:

\begin{equation}
  h(X)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\log f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}\right)
  \label{eq:de}
\end{equation}
The released DE features were smoothed over time with a linear dynamical system (de\_LDS)\cite{seed}, which reduces window-to-window noise but also increases the correlation between neighbouring windows of a trial. For each window the 62 × 5 DE matrix was flattened into a 310-dimensional vector. Each feature \emph{j} was z-standardised,

\begin{equation}
  \tilde{x}_j=\frac{x_j-\mu_j}{\sigma_j},\qquad j=1,\ldots,d
  \label{eq:zscore}
\end{equation}
where μ\textsubscript{j} and σ\textsubscript{j} are the mean and standard deviation of feature \emph{j}. No further normalisation was applied.


\subsection{Electrode montages}
Two electrode configurations were compared. The full montage used all 62 electrodes (310 features). The wearable-compatible montage used six electrodes reachable by a forehead-and-temple band, FP1, FP2, F7, F8, T7 and T8, giving 30 features (6 electrodes × 5 bands). The six-electrode features are an exact column subset of the full feature matrix (indices 0, 2, 5, 13, 23 and 31 of the 62-channel ordering), so the two configurations differ only in electrode density and not in preprocessing.


\subsection{Data augmentation and class balancing}
Data augmentation was used to create the two views required by contrastive pretraining. For a standardised window \emph{x}, the first view adds isotropic Gaussian noise, and the second view masks whole electrodes across all five bands with a random binary mask \emph{m}, each electrode being kept with probability 0.7:

\begin{equation}
  v_1=x+\varepsilon,\quad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\,0.1^{2}I);\qquad v_2=x\odot(m\otimes\mathbf{1}_5),\quad m_c\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.7)
  \label{eq:aug}
\end{equation}
The noise view encourages invariance to small amplitude perturbations, and the masking view encourages invariance to missing or poorly contacting electrodes, a frequent problem with wearable headsets. Augmentation was applied on the fly to each mini-batch during stage 1 only; supervised fine-tuning and evaluation used unaugmented windows. To counter the mild class imbalance, training mini-batches were drawn with a class-balanced sampler whose sampling weight for a window of class \emph{i} is proportional to 1/\emph{n}\textsubscript{i}.


\subsection{Data splitting and evaluation protocol}
We used leave-one-participant-out (LOSO) cross-validation. In each of 15 folds one participant was held out entirely, and the windows of the remaining 14 were split at random into training (85\%, about 29,810 windows) and validation (15\%, about 5260 windows) sets (Fig.~\ref{fig:split}). The procedure was repeated with seeds 1, 7 and 21, giving 45 runs per model and montage (Table~\ref{tab:protocol}). The complete workflow is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}.

\paragraph{Strategy 1: zero-shot evaluation}
The trained model predicts all 2505 windows of the held-out participant without access to any of their labels or unlabelled EEG (except for PseudoLabel, which uses unlabelled held-out EEG during training by design). This protocol measures true cross-participant generalization.


\paragraph{Strategy 2: few-shot calibration}
Twenty windows of the held-out participant are drawn at random, all network parameters are fine-tuned on them for 10 epochs (Adam, learning rate 10\textsuperscript{−3}), and performance is measured on the remaining 2485 windows. Twenty windows correspond to about 80 s of EEG, or 0.8\% of the participant’s data, and emulate a short user-calibration session.

Because calibration windows were sampled across trials, a calibration window and a test window could come from the same film-clip trial; with temporally smoothed features such windows are correlated, so calibrated estimates may be optimistic\cite{liblock}. Validation windows came from the same participants and trials as training windows, so validation scores were near ceiling (median macro F1 1.00 for supervised models) and early stopping effectively selected late epochs; this does not compromise the independence of the held-out participant.


\subsection{Baseline models}

\subsection{Proposed model: CLISA-EWU}
CLISA-EWU (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}) consists of an encoder \emph{f}\textsubscript{θ}, a projection head \emph{g}\textsubscript{φ} used only during pretraining, and a linear classifier. The encoder maps a window \emph{x} to a 128-dimensional representation \emph{h}, and the projection head maps \emph{h} to a 64-dimensional unit vector \emph{z}:

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth,height=0.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/thesis_fig04_clisa_architecture.png}
  \caption{\textbf{CLISA-EWU architecture.} Stage 1: contrastive pretraining of the encoder and projector with the NT-Xent loss on a Gaussian-noise view and an electrode-masking view of each window (AdamW, cosine annealing). Stage 2: supervised fine-tuning of the encoder and linear classifier with cross-entropy. The model is evaluated zero-shot and after 20-window calibration.}
  \label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

\begin{equation}
  h=f_\theta(x)=\mathrm{ReLU}\Bigl(\mathrm{BN}\bigl(W_2\,\mathrm{Dropout}(\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{BN}(W_1x)))\bigr)\Bigr),\qquad z=\frac{g_\phi(h)}{\lVert g_\phi(h)\rVert_2}
  \label{eq:encoder}
\end{equation}
where \emph{W}\textsubscript{1} ∈ ℝ\textsuperscript{256×d} and \emph{W}\textsubscript{2} ∈ ℝ\textsuperscript{128×256} (\emph{d} = 310 or 30), BN denotes batch normalisation and dropout has rate 0.3. The classifier outputs class probabilities ŷ = softmax(\emph{W}\textsubscript{c}\emph{h} + \emph{b}\textsubscript{c}). Training has two stages. In stage 1, the encoder and projection head are trained with the NT-Xent contrastive loss\cite{simclr} on the two augmented views. For a batch of \emph{N} windows, the views give 2\emph{N} projections, and for a positive pair (\emph{i}, \emph{j}) the loss is

\begin{equation}
  \ell_{i,j} = -\log \frac{\exp\left(\mathrm{sim}(z_i,z_j)/\tau\right)}{\sum_{k=1,\,k\neq i}^{2N}\exp\left(\mathrm{sim}(z_i,z_k)/\tau\right)}
  \label{eq:ntxent}
\end{equation}
where sim(·,·) is cosine similarity and τ = 0.5. In stage 2, the encoder and classifier are fine-tuned with the cross-entropy loss over mini-batches B,

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{C}y_{b,i}\log\hat{y}_{b,i}
  \label{eq:ce}
\end{equation}
Unlike the original CLISA\cite{clisa}, which treats time-aligned responses of different participants to the same stimulus as positives, the stage-1 objective here contrasts augmented views of the same window and does not use participant or stimulus identity.


\subsection{Training algorithm}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:clisa} summarises the complete training and evaluation procedure for one montage, including both evaluation protocols.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{CLISA-EWU leave-one-participant-out training and evaluation}
\label{alg:clisa}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Standardised DE features \emph{X} ∈ ℝ\textsuperscript{N×d}, labels \emph{y}, participant identifiers \emph{s}, seeds \{1, 7, 21\}, calibration budget \emph{m} = 20
\Ensure Zero-shot accuracy A\textsubscript{zs} and calibrated accuracy A\textsubscript{cal} for every seed and held-out participant
\For{each seed \emph{r} ∈ (1, 7, 21)}
\For{each participant \emph{p} = 1, …, 15}
\State D\textsubscript{test} ← windows of participant \emph{p}; D\textsubscript{pool} ← windows of the other 14 participants
\State Split D\textsubscript{pool} at random into D\textsubscript{train} (85\%) and D\textsubscript{val} (15\%)
\State Initialise encoder \emph{f}\textsubscript{θ}, projection head \emph{g}\textsubscript{φ} and classifier \emph{c}\textsubscript{ψ}
\For{epoch = 1, …, 50} \Comment{Stage 1: contrastive pretraining}
\For{each class-balanced mini-batch \emph{x} ⊂ D\textsubscript{train}}
\State \emph{v}\textsubscript{1}, \emph{v}\textsubscript{2} ← noise and electrode-masking views of \emph{x}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:aug}
\State Update θ, φ by minimising the NT-Xent loss of the projections of \emph{v}\textsubscript{1} and \emph{v}\textsubscript{2}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ntxent}
\EndFor
\EndFor
\For{epoch = 1, …, 50} \Comment{Stage 2: supervised fine-tuning}
\State Update θ, ψ by minimising the cross-entropy loss on D\textsubscript{train}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ce}
\If{validation macro F1 has not improved for 15 epochs}
\State stop training
\EndIf
\EndFor
\State Restore the best (θ, ψ); A\textsubscript{zs}(\emph{r}, \emph{p}) ← accuracy on D\textsubscript{test} \Comment{zero-shot}
\State Draw \emph{m} windows D\textsubscript{cal} ⊂ D\textsubscript{test} at random; D\textsubscript{eval} ← D\textsubscript{test} ∖ D\textsubscript{cal}
\State Fine-tune θ, ψ on D\textsubscript{cal} for 10 epochs (Adam, learning rate 10\textsuperscript{−3})
\State A\textsubscript{cal}(\emph{r}, \emph{p}) ← accuracy on D\textsubscript{eval} \Comment{calibrated}
\EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return A\textsubscript{zs} and A\textsubscript{cal} for all seeds and participants
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{Implementation details}
Stage 1 uses AdamW (learning rate 10\textsuperscript{−3}, weight decay 10\textsuperscript{−4}, cosine annealing, 50 epochs, batch size 128). Stage 2 uses AdamW (learning rate 3 × 10\textsuperscript{−4}, weight decay 10\textsuperscript{−4}, cosine annealing, up to 50 epochs, early stopping with patience 15 on validation macro F1, class-balanced sampling). Supervised baselines were trained for up to 100 epochs with the same early-stopping rule. Mixed-precision training was used throughout.

All experiments were implemented in Python 3 with PyTorch (deep models), scikit-learn and XGBoost (classical models), and SciPy and statsmodels (statistics), and were run on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 Laptop GPU.


\subsection{Outcomes}
The primary outcome was the accuracy of four-class emotion recognition on the windows of the held-out participant, averaged over three seeds to give one value per participant, under both the zero-shot and the calibrated protocol. Key secondary outcomes were macro F1, the calibration gain (calibrated minus zero-shot accuracy) and electrode-reduction retention. Exploratory outcomes were balanced accuracy, precision, specificity, weighted F1, Cohen’s κ, the Matthews correlation coefficient, the geometric mean of recalls and the information transfer rate (available for CLISA-EWU and DANN), per-class metrics, representation-geometry indices and electrode attributions.


\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
Model performance is assessed with complementary metrics computed on the test windows of the held-out participant. For class \emph{i} of \emph{C} = 4 classes, TP\textsubscript{i}, FP\textsubscript{i}, FN\textsubscript{i} and TN\textsubscript{i} denote true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives, \emph{n}\textsubscript{i} the number of windows of class \emph{i} and \emph{N} the total number of test windows. Accuracy, the metric logged for every run of all models, and per-class specificity are

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{C}TP_i,\qquad \mathrm{Specificity}_i=\frac{TN_i}{TN_i+FP_i}
  \label{eq:acc}
\end{equation}
Per-class recall (sensitivity) and precision are

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Recall}_i=\frac{TP_i}{TP_i+FN_i},\qquad \mathrm{Precision}_i=\frac{TP_i}{TP_i+FP_i}
  \label{eq:recall}
\end{equation}
Balanced accuracy (BA) is the mean per-class recall\cite{brodersen} and removes the influence of class frequency:

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BA}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{i=1}^{C}\mathrm{Recall}_i
  \label{eq:bacc}
\end{equation}
The per-class F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall; macro F1 averages it with equal class weights and weighted F1 with weights proportional to class support:

\begin{equation}
  F1_i=2\cdot\frac{\mathrm{Precision}_i\cdot\mathrm{Recall}_i}{\mathrm{Precision}_i+\mathrm{Recall}_i}
  \label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  F1_{\mathrm{macro}}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{i=1}^{C}F1_i
  \label{eq:macrof1}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  F1_{\mathrm{weighted}}=\sum_{i=1}^{C}\frac{n_i}{N}\,F1_i
  \label{eq:wf1}
\end{equation}
Cohen’s kappa\cite{cohen} corrects accuracy for the agreement expected by chance, where \emph{p}\textsubscript{o} is the observed accuracy and \emph{n}\textsubscript{i·} and \emph{n}\textsubscript{·i} are the row and column totals of the confusion matrix:

\begin{equation}
  \kappa=\frac{p_o-p_e}{1-p_e},\qquad p_e=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{C}n_{i\cdot}\,n_{\cdot i}
  \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
The multi-class Matthews correlation coefficient\cite{gorodkin,chicco} uses every cell of the confusion matrix, with \emph{c} correctly classified windows and \emph{t}\textsubscript{i} and \emph{p}\textsubscript{i} the true and predicted totals of class \emph{i}; it ranges from −1 to 1, with 0 at chance:

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MCC}=\frac{c\,N-\sum_{i}p_i\,t_i}{\sqrt{\left(N^{2}-\sum_{i}p_i^{2}\right)\left(N^{2}-\sum_{i}t_i^{2}\right)}}
  \label{eq:mcc}
\end{equation}
The geometric mean of the recalls\cite{kubat} penalises a model that neglects any class:

\begin{equation}
  G=\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{C}\mathrm{Recall}_i\Bigr)^{1/C}
  \label{eq:gmean}
\end{equation}
To express performance in brain–computer-interface terms, the information transfer rate\cite{wolpaw} is computed from accuracy \emph{P} with window length \emph{T} = 4 s, giving bits per window (\emph{B}) and bits per minute:

\begin{equation}
  B=\log_2 C+P\log_2 P+(1-P)\log_2\frac{1-P}{C-1},\qquad \mathrm{ITR}=B\cdot\frac{60}{T}
  \label{eq:itr}
\end{equation}
Electrode-reduction retention is the ratio of six-electrode to 62-electrode accuracy for the same model:

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Retention}=\frac{\mathrm{Acc}_{6}}{\mathrm{Acc}_{62}}
  \label{eq:retention}
\end{equation}
The run logs stored accuracy and macro F1 for every deep model, and full confusion matrices for CLISA-EWU and DANN. The complete metric set is therefore reported for these two models, and model-wide comparisons use accuracy and macro F1. Because the four classes are nearly balanced (21–27\% of windows), accuracy and balanced accuracy differed by 0.0–0.4 percentage points on average and by at most 2.4 points in any single run. Confusion matrices are row-normalised so that each row shows the recall of one true class.


\subsection{Statistical analysis}
Runs from the same participant are not independent, so the three seeds were averaged for each participant, giving 15 paired observations per model, montage and protocol. Participant-level means are reported with 95\% confidence intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of participants.

Differences among all models within a montage and protocol were tested with the Friedman test\cite{friedman} on participant-level accuracy, with Kendall’s \emph{W} as the effect size. Average ranks $\bar{R}$\textsubscript{j} of \emph{k} models over \emph{n} = 15 participants were compared with the Nemenyi critical difference\cite{demsar}, where \emph{q}\textsubscript{α} is the studentised range statistic divided by √2 at α = 0.05:

\begin{equation}
  \chi^{2}_{F}=\frac{12n}{k(k+1)}\Biggl[\sum_{j=1}^{k}\bar{R}_j^{2}-\frac{k(k+1)^{2}}{4}\Biggr],\qquad W=\frac{\chi^{2}_{F}}{n(k-1)}
  \label{eq:friedman}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CD}=q_{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{k(k+1)}{6n}}
  \label{eq:cd}
\end{equation}
Pairwise comparisons of CLISA-EWU with seven comparators used two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests\cite{wilcoxon} with Holm adjustment\cite{holm} over the 14 comparisons of each protocol, and paired \emph{t}-tests as a parametric check. Effect sizes were Cohen’s \emph{d}\textsubscript{z} for the paired differences \emph{D}\cite{lakens} and the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation \emph{r}\textsubscript{rb}\cite{kerby}, where \emph{T}\textsuperscript{+} and \emph{T}\textsuperscript{−} are the sums of positive and negative signed ranks:

\begin{equation}
  d_z=\frac{\bar{D}}{s_D},\qquad r_{rb}=\frac{T^{+}-T^{-}}{T^{+}+T^{-}}
  \label{eq:effect}
\end{equation}
Variability of CLISA-EWU across participants and seeds was decomposed with a two-way analysis of variance without replication (participant × seed), and reliability across seeds was summarised with the consistency intraclass correlation\cite{shrout}, where MS\textsubscript{P} and MS\textsubscript{E} are the participant and residual mean squares and \emph{k} = 3 seeds:

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ICC}(3,1)=\frac{MS_P-MS_E}{MS_P+(k-1)\,MS_E}
  \label{eq:icc}
\end{equation}
Associations between zero-shot accuracy, calibration gain and montage were assessed with Spearman’s ρ. The 62- versus six-electrode difference was tested with the Wilcoxon test, and the confidence interval for retention was obtained by resampling participants. Analyses used Python 3 with SciPy and statsmodels, and the scripts are provided in the repository. Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI where applicable\cite{tripod}.


\subsection{Sample size and statistical power}
The number of participants was fixed at 15 by the dataset, so a sensitivity power analysis was performed instead of an a priori sample-size calculation. With 15 paired participant-level observations, a two-sided paired test at α = 0.05 has 80\% power to detect a standardised effect of \emph{d}\textsubscript{z} = 0.78, and 90\% power for \emph{d}\textsubscript{z} = 0.90; at a Bonferroni-level α of 0.05/14, 80\% power requires \emph{d}\textsubscript{z} = 1.15. Given the observed standard deviation of paired differences between CLISA-EWU and DANN after calibration, \emph{d}\textsubscript{z} = 0.78 corresponds to about 1.5 percentage points with 62 electrodes and 3.3 points with six electrodes. Smaller differences between the best models cannot be resolved reliably with this dataset, and non-significant comparisons should be read as inconclusive rather than as evidence of equality unless supported by the equivalence analysis.


\subsection{Sensitivity analyses and missing data}
Six pre-planned sensitivity analyses tested the robustness of the main comparisons. (i) Linear mixed-effects models\cite{laird} fitted to run-level accuracy, with model and seed as fixed effects and a random intercept and random model effect for each participant, so that all 45 runs were used without treating them as independent. (ii) A leave-one-participant-out jackknife\cite{efron}, recomputing each paired comparison and the retention ratio 15 times with one participant removed. (iii) Seed-wise analyses, repeating the ranking and the comparison with DANN separately for each seed. (iv) Re-analysis of the CLISA-EWU versus DANN comparison with balanced accuracy computed from the confusion matrices. (v) Two one-sided tests (TOST)\cite{schuirmann} of equivalence between zero-shot CLISA-EWU and the two perceptron baselines with margins of ±2 and ±3 percentage points. (vi) The probability that each model is the best, estimated as the proportion of 10,000 participant bootstrap resamples\cite{efron} in which it had the highest mean accuracy.

All 2,250 runs (1,350 deep and 900 classical) completed, so there were no missing data and no imputation was required.


\subsection{Representation and attribution analysis}
Representation geometry was examined with t-SNE\cite{tsne} and with silhouette\cite{silhouette}, Davies–Bouldin\cite{dbi} and Calinski–Harabasz\cite{chi} indices computed on PCA-reduced raw features and on CLISA-EWU encoder embeddings, using emotion and participant labels. Electrode importance was estimated with gradient-based SHAP\cite{shap} on the 62-electrode model of the best run, summing absolute attributions over the five bands of each electrode separately for each predicted class.

\subsection{Zero-shot cross-participant decoding}
Without target data, no model approached the accuracies reported in much of the literature (Fig.~\ref{fig:zs}). With 62 electrodes, CLISA-EWU had the highest mean zero-shot accuracy (0.485, 95\% CI 0.428–0.542) but did not differ from the two-layer perceptron (difference 0.9 percentage points, −0.5 to 2.1; Wilcoxon p = 0.25) or the deep perceptron (0.6 points, −0.9 to 2.2; p = 0.64). Its advantage over DANN (2.7 points, 1.0 to 4.2; unadjusted p = 0.010) did not survive Holm correction (p = 0.12). With six electrodes, zero-shot accuracy fell to 0.406 (0.370–0.439) for CLISA-EWU, and SimCLR (0.446) and BYOL (0.440) were numerically higher (both Holm-adjusted p = 1.00). Zero-shot performance varied greatly between participants, from 0.244 (participant 11, at chance) to 0.723 (participant 7) for CLISA-EWU with 62 electrodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:subj}a).

\subsection{Effect of few-shot calibration}
Calibration with 20 labelled windows changed the ranking of models (Fig.~\ref{fig:zs}, Table~\ref{tab:results}). Models trained with a contrastive, domain-adversarial or pseudo-label objective (CLISA-EWU, SimCLR, BYOL, DANN, PseudoLabel) gained 15.6–21.4 percentage points (MixMatch and the DANCE pair gained 9.4–25.0 points), whereas purely supervised networks gained 0.1–2.0 points under the identical calibration routine. After calibration CLISA-EWU had the highest mean accuracy with both montages: 0.678 (0.641–0.718) with 62 electrodes and 0.620 (0.590–0.648) with six (Fig.~\ref{fig:violin}). The mechanism behind the objective-dependent gain is not established here; candidate explanations include adaptation of batch-normalisation statistics to the new participant\cite{adabn} and differences in how sharply each objective fits the training participants.

\subsection{Ranking across participants}
Friedman tests showed significant differences among the 15 deep models in every montage and protocol (Table~\ref{tab:friedman}; all p < 0.001). Agreement between participants on the ordering of models was weak zero-shot (Kendall’s W = 0.35 for both montages) but strong after calibration (W = 0.79 and 0.77), so calibration made the ranking consistent across people (Fig.~\ref{fig:cd}). After calibration, CLISA-EWU had the best average rank with 62 electrodes (1.40) and with six (2.67). The Nemenyi critical difference for 15 models and 15 participants was 5.54 ranks; by this conservative criterion, calibrated CLISA-EWU was distinguishable from every supervised network, MixMatch and the DANCE pair, but not from DANN (2.40), PseudoLabel (2.53), BYOL (4.07) or SimCLR (5.80) with 62 electrodes. Zero-shot, CLISA-EWU ranked first with 62 electrodes (4.53), but the deep and two-layer perceptrons were within 0.4 ranks of it, and with six electrodes it ranked third (5.93) behind BYOL (4.13) and SimCLR (4.87).

\subsection{Effect sizes}
Paired effect sizes (Table~\ref{tab:paired}) separate statistical from practical significance. After calibration with 62 electrodes, the advantage of CLISA-EWU over BYOL and SimCLR was very large (\emph{d}\textsubscript{z} = 2.04 and 2.62; \emph{r}\textsubscript{rb} = 1.00, with all 15 participants favouring CLISA-EWU), whereas its advantage over PseudoLabel and DANN was large in standardised terms (\emph{d}\textsubscript{z} = 0.98 and 0.82) but small in absolute terms (2.1 and 1.6 percentage points), and only the PseudoLabel difference survived Holm correction. With six electrodes, effects against the self-supervised and semi-supervised models were small (\emph{d}\textsubscript{z} = 0.14–0.30) and non-significant. Zero-shot, CLISA-EWU differed meaningfully only from EEG Conformer with six electrodes (5.3 points, \emph{d}\textsubscript{z} = 1.38, Holm p = 0.001); its differences from the perceptrons were negligible (\emph{d}\textsubscript{z} = 0.14–0.32).


\subsection{Electrode reduction}
Reducing the montage from 62 to six electrodes lowered calibrated accuracy for CLISA-EWU by 5.8 percentage points (Wilcoxon p < 0.001), a retention of 91.4\% (95\% CI 87.9–95.0\%; Fig.~\ref{fig:subj}b, Fig.~\ref{fig:core}a). Zero-shot retention was lower, at 83.6\%. Retention differed between models (Fig.~\ref{fig:core}b): BYOL and SimCLR performed as well or better with six electrodes than with 62, whereas EEG Conformer retained 78.5\%. After calibration, every six-electrode contrastive, domain-adversarial and pseudo-label model remained above chance for every participant (CLISA-EWU minimum 0.518).

\subsection{Multi-metric evaluation}
Table~\ref{tab:cmext} reports the complete confusion-matrix metric set for CLISA-EWU and DANN. Calibrated CLISA-EWU with 62 electrodes reached an accuracy of 0.678, balanced accuracy 0.676, macro F1 0.673, Cohen’s κ 0.569 (95\% CI 0.519–0.622) and MCC 0.577; with six electrodes the corresponding values were 0.620, 0.615, 0.611, 0.490 (0.449–0.530) and 0.499. On the Landis–Koch scale\cite{landis}, calibrated agreement beyond chance was moderate (κ 0.41–0.60) for both montages, whereas zero-shot agreement was fair with 62 electrodes (κ = 0.31) and at the border of slight and fair with six (κ = 0.21). MCC tracked κ to within 0.01, macro and weighted F1 differed by at most 0.004, and the geometric mean of recalls (0.659 and 0.594 after calibration) stayed close to balanced accuracy, so the results were not driven by class imbalance or by neglect of any class. Mean specificity was high (0.893 and 0.873), indicating that errors were spread across classes rather than concentrated on one. Expressed as an information transfer rate, calibration raised throughput from 3.35 to 9.13 bits min\textsuperscript{−1} with 62 electrodes and from 1.51 to 6.85 bits min\textsuperscript{−1} with six. CLISA-EWU exceeded DANN on every metric in both montages and protocols, by 1.2–3.8 points of κ.

\subsection{Who benefits from calibration}
Calibration gain was negatively correlated with zero-shot accuracy (Spearman ρ = −0.60, p = 0.017 with 62 electrodes; ρ = −0.58, p = 0.024 with six; Fig.~\ref{fig:gain}a): participants who were decoded worst without calibration gained most, up to 39 percentage points for participant 11. Part of this association is expected from regression to the mean, because the gain contains the zero-shot score. Across montages, participants’ calibrated accuracies were strongly correlated (ρ = 0.80, p < 0.001) whereas their zero-shot accuracies were not (ρ = −0.05, p = 0.86; Fig.~\ref{fig:gain}b), so after calibration a participant who is decoded well with 62 electrodes is also decoded well with six. Across models, participant rankings before and after calibration were almost identical for supervised networks (ρ = 0.94–1.00) but less so for the adaptable models (ρ = 0.43–0.93), consistent with calibration substantively changing only the latter.

In the jackknife, the calibrated advantage of CLISA-EWU with 62 electrodes ranged from 1.4 to 1.9 points over DANN and from 1.9 to 2.4 points over PseudoLabel across the 15 leave-one-out subsets, and unadjusted Wilcoxon p values remained below 0.05 in every subset; with six electrodes, the difference from BYOL ranged from 0.0 to 1.4 points and was never significant (p ≥ 0.39). Retention ranged narrowly from 90.7\% to 92.3\%, so no single participant drove the electrode-reduction result. CLISA-EWU had the highest calibrated mean accuracy with 62 electrodes for each of the three seeds and with six electrodes for two of three seeds (BYOL ranked first with seed 7), and its advantage over DANN varied between seeds (0.8–2.5 points with 62 electrodes). Using balanced accuracy instead of accuracy gave the same pattern for CLISA-EWU versus DANN (calibrated: +1.8 points with 62 electrodes, p = 0.007, and +2.9 points with six, p = 0.022).

The equivalence analysis supported the first hypothesis. The 90\% confidence interval for the zero-shot difference between CLISA-EWU and the two-layer perceptron was −0.4 to 2.1 points with 62 electrodes, and the two models were statistically equivalent within a margin of ±3 points (TOST p = 0.004; six electrodes, p = 0.009) but not within ±2 points (p = 0.06). In the bootstrap, the probability that CLISA-EWU was the best calibrated model was above 99.9\% with 62 electrodes but only 63\% with six electrodes, where BYOL (26\%) and PseudoLabel (9\%) were credible alternatives; zero-shot, CLISA-EWU had a 72\% probability of being best with 62 electrodes, whereas with six electrodes SimCLR (70\%) and BYOL (27\%) were the most likely best models. Finally, the calibration gain of CLISA-EWU exceeded 10 points for 13 of 15 participants with 62 electrodes and for all 15 with six electrodes (ranges 2.8–39.4 and 13.9–35.4 points).


\subsection{Principal findings}
This study provides a controlled, participant-level comparison of 26 models for cross-participant EEG emotion recognition. Three principal findings emerged, each supporting one of the hypotheses. First, without any data from the new user, four-class accuracy was modest for every model, and the best deep model was statistically equivalent to a two-layer perceptron within ±3 percentage points. Second, about 80 s of labelled calibration data raised accuracy by roughly 20 points, but only for models trained with contrastive, adversarial or pseudo-label objectives; supervised networks barely changed. Third, a six-electrode frontal–temporal montage retained about 91\% of calibrated full-montage accuracy, and participants who were decoded well with 62 electrodes were also decoded well with six. These findings were stable across mixed-effects, jackknife, seed-wise, balanced-accuracy and equivalence analyses.


\subsection{Comparison with previous studies}
Our calibrated accuracies (0.678 with 62 electrodes) fall within the range reported for cross-participant SEED-IV studies, whereas our zero-shot accuracies (0.485) fall below it (Table~\ref{tab:lit}). Many of the higher published values were obtained with domain-adaptation methods that use unlabelled or labelled data from the test participant\cite{dan,bidann,rgnn,plugplay}, which is consistent with our observation that target data, rather than architecture, accounts for most of the measurable gain. Our findings also agree with the brain–computer-interface literature, in which a short per-user calibration is standard and transfer learning mainly serves to shorten it\cite{lotte,jayaram,wu}. The original CLISA reported strong cross-participant performance using inter-participant alignment of time-locked responses\cite{clisa}; our two-stage model used single-window augmentations instead, and its zero-shot equivalence with a perceptron suggests that this form of contrastive pretraining alone does not produce participant-invariant representations.

The electrode-reduction results extend the subject-dependent findings of Zheng and Lu, who showed that small lateral temporal subsets retained most of the information in SEED\cite{seed}, to the cross-participant setting. The attribution results, which placed the most informative electrodes for fear over parieto-occipital cortex, are consistent with reports that emotion-related activity is not confined to frontal sites\cite{alarcao,jenke}, and they explain why fear recall dropped most when the montage was reduced.

The calibrated mean of CLISA-EWU (67.8\%) lies within the range of published cross-participant SEED-IV results (Table~\ref{tab:lit}), and the zero-shot mean (48.5\%) lies below it. Because the published values differ in their use of target-participant data and in reporting unit, they cannot be ranked against the present results; a fair comparison requires re-evaluating published architectures under both protocols used here.


\subsection{Possible mechanisms}
Why calibration helped some models much more than others is not established by this study, but three mechanisms are plausible. First, calibration updates batch-normalisation statistics with data from the new participant, which is itself an effective form of domain adaptation\cite{adabn}; all deep models had batch normalisation, so this alone cannot explain the difference. Second, the supervised networks reached near-perfect validation scores on windows from the training participants, suggesting sharp, overconfident decision boundaries that ten epochs at a small learning rate could not move, whereas contrastive and adversarial objectives produce smoother representations that a few labelled samples can re-align. Third, the pseudo-label and adversarial objectives explicitly reduce reliance on participant-specific features, which may leave more class information available for rapid re-weighting. Distinguishing these mechanisms requires the calibration ablation and the trial-held-out protocol described in the future work.


\subsection{Strengths and limitations}
The study has several strengths. All 26 models were evaluated under an identical protocol with the same splits, seeds and features, removing the protocol differences that confound comparisons between published studies. Every result was summarised at the participant level, the unit of generalisation, with bootstrap confidence intervals, paired non-parametric tests with multiplicity correction, effect sizes, rank-based omnibus tests and six sensitivity analyses, including an equivalence test that allows a positive conclusion of no meaningful difference. The evaluation reported both zero-shot and calibrated performance, a broad set of complementary metrics, variance decomposition and class-level behaviour. Errors found during the analysis, including a mislabelled metric and an invalid statistical test in the earlier thesis draft, were corrected and documented, and all per-fold outputs and analysis scripts are openly available.

This study has limitations. The cohort was small (15 participants), healthy and drawn from one laboratory, and emotions were elicited with film clips under controlled conditions; generalisation to other populations, naturalistic settings and clinical groups is unknown\cite{seed,seedv,faced}. The six-electrode montage was simulated by selecting channels from a laboratory cap rather than recorded with a wearable device. Calibration windows were sampled across trials, so calibration and test windows could share a trial, which may have inflated calibrated accuracy\cite{liblock}. Labels were assigned per film clip rather than per moment. We used precomputed features, so preprocessing choices could not be varied, and hyperparameters were not tuned separately for each model. Finally, the contrastive model contrasted augmented views of single windows rather than aligning participants explicitly, so its gains should not be attributed to inter-participant alignment.

The sample of 15 participants also limits statistical power: differences smaller than about 1.5 percentage points with 62 electrodes and 3.3 points with six electrodes between the leading models could not be resolved reliably, so non-significant comparisons among them are inconclusive. Finally, the confusion-matrix metrics could be computed only for the two models whose run logs stored confusion matrices.


\subsection{Implications for research and practice}
These findings change how cross-participant results should be read. Much of the published progress on SEED-IV has been attributed to architectural innovation, yet our results suggest that the protocol, and specifically whether and how target-participant data are used, may explain as much variance as the architecture. Under calibration, the ordering of model families inverted: supervised networks that matched self-supervised models zero-shot fell far behind them. Comparisons that mix zero-shot, transductive and calibrated results, or that compare a best fold with another study's mean, are therefore uninterpretable. We recommend that cross-participant EEG studies report zero-shot and calibrated results separately, specify the number and selection of calibration samples, keep calibration and test trials disjoint, and summarise results at the participant level with confidence intervals\cite{tripod,kapoor}.

For research, the results indicate that cross-participant EEG emotion recognition should be reported with five elements: zero-shot and calibrated results reported separately; the number, selection and timing of calibration samples; calibration and test data drawn from disjoint trials; participant-level summaries with confidence intervals and paired tests; and per-fold outputs made available. Without these, differences between published methods cannot be attributed to the methods themselves\cite{tripod,kapoor,demsar}.

For practice, a short calibration session is acceptable in most consumer and research workflows, and a headband carrying frontal and temporal electrodes appears to retain most of the decodable signal under calibration. Because the seed-dependent choice of calibration windows accounted for up to 9\% of the variance in accuracy, averaging over several calibration draws or selecting calibration trials systematically would make deployed systems more stable. Nevertheless, the absolute calibrated accuracy (about 62\% for four classes with six electrodes) and the large between-participant spread mean that individual predictions remain unreliable, and the system is not suitable for clinical decision-making.


\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
In this benchmark of 26 models under one leave-one-participant-out protocol, three findings stand out. First, decoding four emotions in an unseen participant without any of their data was modest for every model, and the deep architectures, including the contrastive model of primary interest, did not clearly outperform a two-layer perceptron. Second, about 80 s of labelled calibration data raised accuracy by roughly 20 percentage points, but only for models trained with contrastive, domain-adversarial or pseudo-label objectives. Third, a six-electrode frontal–temporal montage retained about nine-tenths of calibrated full-montage performance. In a controlled participant-independent benchmark, brief labelled calibration rather than architecture accounted for most of the measurable gain in EEG emotion recognition, and a six-electrode wearable-compatible montage retained about 91\% of calibrated performance. Rigorous separation of zero-shot and calibrated evaluation, external validation and real wearable recordings are the next steps towards dependable EEG-based affect sensing.

Future work will address the limitations identified above:

\begin{itemize}
  \item External validation on SEED, SEED-V and FACED, and training on SEED-IV with testing on these datasets without retraining\cite{seed,seedv,faced}.
  \item A trial-held-out calibration protocol (calibrate on session 1, test on sessions 2–3) and a calibration-budget curve of 5–200 windows to remove the trial-overlap optimism of the present calibration.
  \item An ablation that calibrates only batch-normalisation statistics, only the classifier, or all parameters, to identify the mechanism behind the objective-dependent calibration gain.
  \item A true inter-participant contrastive objective with stimulus-aligned positives from different participants, to test whether zero-shot accuracy can be improved.
  \item A seven- or eight-electrode montage that adds a parieto-occipital site, motivated by the attribution results for fear.
  \item Recording a new cohort with a dry-electrode wearable headband and real-time deployment of the NeuroSync interface, followed by studies in clinically relevant populations.
\end{itemize}
